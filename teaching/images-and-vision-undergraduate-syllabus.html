Images and Vision: From Pixels to Deep Learning

Level: Undergraduate  ·  Duration: 13 weeks, one session per week  ·  Lecturer: Dr. Alexander (Sasha) Apartsin

Course text: Building Vision AI: From Pixels to Generative Models (open access) · ebook at Amazon

Course Description
This course is a hands-on, code-first introduction to computer vision, tracing the field from the physics of image formation to modern deep learning. It begins with how images are formed and represented, then builds through classical image processing (filtering, the frequency domain, morphology, and enhancement), classical computer vision (features, segmentation, camera geometry, stereo, motion, and 3D reconstruction), and finally deep vision (convolutional networks, detection, and segmentation). Throughout, concepts are demonstrated with practical coding examples using contemporary Python libraries, and are reinforced by a semester-long project in which students build, evaluate, and iterate on a vision system of their own.

Prerequisites

All necessary background material, including a refresher on machine learning fundamentals and the PyTorch library, is introduced during the course. Students are expected to be proficient in Python programming and comfortable with basic linear algebra, calculus, and probability. Prior exposure to machine learning is helpful but not required.

Expected Outcomes

Students who complete the course will acquire a broad, in-depth understanding of computer vision, from classical image processing to modern deep learning. By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  • Explain how digital images are formed, sampled, and represented, and apply core image-processing operations (filtering, frequency-domain analysis, morphology, and enhancement).
  • Apply classical computer-vision techniques, including feature detection and description, segmentation, camera calibration, stereo, optical flow, and 3D reconstruction.
  • Design, train, and evaluate deep vision models for image classification, object detection, and segmentation using PyTorch.
  • Build and iterate on an end-to-end vision system through a structured, guided project, combining and extending existing components.
  • Present and articulate technical work with confidence through in-class project presentations.

Course Format

The course runs for 13 weeks with one session per week. Ten sessions are lectures on the week's topic, taught with a code-first approach through hands-on examples using contemporary libraries and tools. The remaining three sessions (Weeks 5, 9, and 13) are dedicated entirely to student project presentations: proposal, interim, and final. In all three, teams present and receive in-class feedback from the instructor and peers.

Course Project

During the course, students will propose, implement, and present a project that builds an end-to-end vision system on a task of their choice. The project runs across the semester as a sequence of milestones: assembling the repository and team, scoping the problem, building a baseline, evaluating the model under image distortions and enhancements, and improving and fine-tuning the model with a documented analysis of what changed and why. For inspiration, a sample of past student projects supervised by the instructor is available at apartsin.com/teaching/student-projects. The milestones, all presented in class, are:

  • Project proposal (Week 5). A talk covering the chosen problem, its motivation, the proposed approach, and the experimental plan. Presented for feedback and approval.
  • Interim progress update (Week 9). A talk with first experimental results, what is and is not working, and the plan for the remaining weeks.
  • Final presentation (Week 13). A talk covering the contribution, method, experiments, results, and conclusions.
  • Project repository. A GitHub repository containing all project materials, including presentation slides, source code, datasets, and a detailed README file.

Policies

Use of AI tools

The use of AI tools for generating project code and presentation slides is highly encouraged, provided that two essential requirements are met: the project must demonstrate novelty by addressing a new and valuable task, and the team must maintain full ownership and responsibility for all submitted code and presentation materials.

Collaboration and integrity

Discussion across teams is encouraged; the code, experiments, and writing submitted by each team must be its own. All results reported in presentations and documentation must be backed by runnable artifacts in the team's repository.
