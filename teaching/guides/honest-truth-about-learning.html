The skills you choose, and how you build them, shape your career more than any single course does. Start by deciding what to build, then face the hard truths about how a skill is actually gained, and why only practice and honest feedback get you there.

The decision, and five hard truths

Choose your skills deliberately, then face why only practice and honest feedback build them.

1

Decide and shape your skillset

Decide which skills you want to acquire: building systems in a domain, explaining concepts to your peers, inventing new product concepts, understanding and modifying code quickly, training new models. Make the decision explicit and write it down. This is one of the most important decisions of your career, so do not let others make it for you, and do not let a course syllabus or an exam format decide it. Research which skills are in demand, and explore what you are good at and what you enjoy.

2

Aim for skills, not passive knowledge

Tell skills apart from passive knowledge. Passive knowledge sits locked in your head: hard to use, hard to show others, and hard to assess. Aim instead for practical skills that produce tangible, demonstrable results.

3

The only way to get better is to practice

You improve a skill by practicing it, not by hearing lectures or reading about it. To play the guitar you have to practice; to build systems, explain concepts, or invent products, you have to practice, and a lot. Grab every opportunity to do so.

4

School will not tell you how good you really are

The path from school through university is not built to tell you honestly how well you have mastered a skill. Negative feedback is unpleasant to give, and detailed, informative feedback takes time, so some students end up with an illusion of mastery and no incentive to improve. In a job interview, when a team lead has to stake their own standing on whether they can rely on you, that illusion gets tested, and the candidate can be turned down, often again without real feedback.

5

Instructors and interviewers see more than they say

Your instructors and interviewers understand far more than they let on. After enough years it becomes obvious what a student actually did, where they cut corners, and where they repeat words they do not truly understand. Nobody is obliged to expose it, so a student can keep the illusion that they can work around the gaps and talk their way through. It works during your studies, but it usually fails in real work.

6

AI, primed correctly, is your honest mirror

The remedy for all of the above is AI. Primed the right way, it can ask you questions, give honest criticism, advise you, and help you improve. Get good at using it that way.
