AI Just Pushed Us Into the Post-Content Era

Content creation got cheap. Ideas did not. Time the web noticed the difference.

Somewhere right now, someone has the idea that would fix your problem. You will never find it, because it is buried inside an article nobody will read.

It has happened before. In 1866, a monk named Gregor Mendel showed how traits pass from parents to children, and published it in the journal of a small local science society. Almost nobody read it. In 34 years it was cited only a handful of times. In 1900, botanists working separately found the same thing, and only then dug up his paper. Genetics lost a generation.

Now imagine 1866 with a Web of Ideas. Mendel’s three sentences get a permanent address. The first farmer who asks why a trait skips a generation finds them, along with who supports them and who contradicts them. In 1900, the botanists don’t rediscover the idea. They extend it.

Mendel did not need a better article. He needed his idea to have an address. That is the Web of Ideas. It does not exist yet. AI just made it possible.

Ideas Have No Address

Today, the only way to share an idea is to wrap it in a story: an article, a deck, a paper, a video. The idea itself has no address. It lives scattered across the packages that carry it, and you cannot ask the web where it came from, what it builds on, or who challenged it.

The web can tell you which pages mention an idea. It cannot tell you where the idea stands.

A polished article used to prove that someone had done the thinking. AI broke that proof: hand it any idea, good or bad, and it hands back the article, the explainer, and the slide deck, equally polished.

AI made writing free. It did not make thinking free.

Keep the Idea. Generate the Narrative.

People still need stories to understand ideas. They just don’t need the same story. Once AI writes the narrative, the idea stays fixed and the telling changes for each reader: a short version for a newcomer, a proof for an expert, a chart for a visual thinker, a debate for a skeptic.

But look at what our tools actually store:

  • Stories are one telling for one audience: articles, videos, posts.
  • Facts are settled, with one version: names, dates, measurements.
  • Ideas are open claims: they survive any retelling, and reasonable people can still disagree with them.
Search engines index stories. Knowledge graphs store facts. Nothing stores ideas.

The Seed: An Idea With an Address

The page was the unit of the first web. The seed is the unit of this one: a claim, not a topic, in one to three sentences, stated so it could turn out wrong. Mendel’s idea is one.

Each seed carries a few fields: the question it answers, its evidence, its author and history, and a status: open, supported, contested, or settled.

Seeds connect through typed links: builds on, supports, contradicts, refines, applies to, same idea as. Seed, link, seed: that is this web’s hyperlink.

Publish the seed. Let AI grow the tree.

Why Now: AI Does the Hard Part

This was tried before. In 2001, Tim Berners-Lee and colleagues described a Semantic Web of pages that carry meaning computers can read. It took hold in niches, but it asked people to write formal markup by hand, and most never did.

The Web of Ideas asks for three sentences. AI does the rest:

  • Deduplicating. Before a seed goes live, AI checks whether the idea already exists in other words. If it does, you extend that seed with your evidence or example, and your part stays credited to you. One idea, one address.
  • Connecting. It proposes links and evidence, and flags when a seed from botany answers a question from medicine.
  • Judging. A seed becomes supported as evidence piles up, contested when a strong counter-seed appears, and settled when the challenges stop landing. Status is earned, not declared.
  • Telling. It turns any seed, or a whole branch of them, into an article, video, or podcast on demand.

Every part exists today: search by meaning, knowledge graphs, claim checking, media generation. What is missing is the plumbing that points them at ideas instead of pages. Call it the IdeaStack, and build it as an open protocol, not a platform. If one company owns the idea graph, it owns how the world thinks.

On the content web, the best-told version wins. On the Web of Ideas, the best-supported idea wins.

Fair Questions

Is writing dead? No. Writing is how you find an idea. Publishing is how you share it. Only the second one changes.

Won’t AI spam seeds? It will. But a bad seed is exposed in three sentences. A bad article can hide for three thousand words.

Who decides how ideas link? Anyone can propose a link, anyone can challenge it, and every link records who made it.

How Ideas Become Facts

Ideas are where facts come from. In 1866, Mendel’s “separate pieces” were an open claim. Today we call them genes. On the Web of Ideas, that whole journey sits at one address, and you can watch it flip from open to settled.

The first web connected documents. The social web connected people. The next one connects ideas. A student starts at the edge of human knowledge, not on page one of a search result. A monk’s pea experiments reach every farmer who needs them, the same year. If you have an idea, you no longer owe the world an article. You owe it the idea.

Here is this whole essay as a seed:

  1. When AI can write the article, the idea, not the article, is the contribution.
  2. Yet the web still stores and searches content, so ideas are hard to find, trace, and build on.
  3. So build a Web of Ideas: publish ideas as linked seeds, and let AI merge duplicates, propose links, weigh evidence, and tell each one as an article, video, or podcast on demand.

Everything else you read was the tree.

The Internet connected what people wrote. The Web of Ideas will connect what people think. Welcome.
