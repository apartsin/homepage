The question everyone is asking

Will AI make software engineers obsolete?

Nobody knows.

The technology moves too fast for confident predictions. But we can make an educated guess by looking at:

  • What AI can already do today
  • What happened in earlier technology shifts
But first, what is this job really?

Software is the closest thing we have to a superpower.

You describe an idea, in words or in code, and a machine turns it into something real. One person with a laptop can build a thing that millions of people use.

Why people fall in love with it
💡

Design / Innovate

You make things that did not exist yesterday. Nothing, then something, from pure thought.

🧩

Build / Puzzles

It is puzzles and detective work. Then the moment it finally clicks and the whole thing comes alive.

🌍

Your software changes the world

Medicine, science, space, how billions of people talk. Software quietly runs the modern world.

This is the thing AI is supposedly making obsolete. Keep that in mind as we look at the futures.

Three possible futures
Future 1

AI is the software

AI becomes the interface itself.

Future 2

AI builds it all

From idea to finished product, autonomously.

Future 3

Engineers essential

The job survives, but changes shape.

Future 1

AI is the software

Why click through menus and spreadsheets if you can simply tell an AI what you want?

Some of today's software may disappear, because AI becomes the interface itself.

Future 2

AI builds software autonomously

A person describes a need. AI does the rest, end to end.

The code underneath may end up written for machines, not for humans to read.

The extreme version of today's "vibe coding." More on that soon.

Future 3 · the one many bet on

Engineers stay essential.
The job changes.

AI becomes an indispensable tool and a huge productivity multiplier. But someone still has to decide:

  • What should be built?
  • How should the system behave?
  • Which trade-offs are acceptable?
  • Is the AI-produced solution actually correct?
  • Is it secure, reliable, maintainable, useful?

Many people in the industry see this as the most plausible future.

Wait. Cheaper software means fewer jobs, right?

If AI makes each programmer more productive, we need fewer programmers.

If software becomes much cheaper to create, we may create much more of it.

  • Today: companies bend themselves to a few ready-made products. Custom software costs too much.
  • If AI slashes that cost: even a tiny company can afford software built for its own way of working.
This already happened once

Once websites became cheap to build, everyone wanted their own custom one.

We did not end up needing fewer websites. Every shop, band, club, and person wanted a site tailored to them. Software may follow the same path.

Another precedent: photography

A professional once needed film, chemicals, exposure timing, a darkroom.

Digital cameras automated nearly all of that.

Photography did not disappear. The skill moved.

The craft shifted toward
  • Composition
  • Lighting
  • Storytelling
  • Selection
  • Artistic judgment
The same shift, for software

What used to make you strong

  • Mastering one technology stack
  • Writing large amounts of code by hand
  • Memorizing exact commands and syntax
  • Debugging tiny implementation details

These skills will not vanish, but AI shrinks their relative importance.

The engineer moves from producing every detail personally toward directing, evaluating, integrating, and improving what AI produces.

And here is the good news

AI takes the tedious work. The creative work gets bigger.

The wiring, the boilerplate, the syntax you had to memorize: AI handles more of it every year. That leaves more of your time for the human part. Deciding what to build, designing how it feels, exercising taste and judgment.

So what will make an engineer strong?

Four skills worth building

Not a survival checklist. These are what let one creative person build what used to take a whole team.

01

Breadth

One person now covers what once took several specialists.

02

Clear technical communication

Say precisely what should be built.

03

Judgment under uncertainty

Evaluate work in territory you have never seen.

04

Fast learning

Today's tools are not next year's tools.

Skill 01 · Breadth

One head, many hats

A young engineer may need to think, at the same time, about:

🏗️ Architecture
🗄️ Databases
🎨 User experience
🧪 Testing
🔒 Security
🚀 Deployment
⚡ Performance
🤖 AI
📋 Product requirements

You do not need to be world-class in all of them. You need enough to recognize what matters, and to notice when something is wrong. That reach is the fun part: you get to build the whole thing.

Skill 02 · Clear technical communication

What, exactly, are we trying to build?

You will communicate with AI systems, customers, managers, designers, and other engineers. Precision is the job.

In an AI-assisted world, vague thinking quickly becomes vague software.

Skill 03 · Judgment under uncertainty

Grading homework in a subject you never took

AI can hand you solutions built with technologies you have never seen. You need enough fundamentals and breadth to:

  1. Understand what AI produced
  2. Spot suspicious assumptions
  3. Compare alternatives
  4. Decide what deserves a closer look
  5. Steer the system in the right direction
Skill 04 · Fast learning

The tools you learn today may be gone in five years.
Maybe two.

So the ability to learn quickly becomes a central professional skill: new AI tools, new technologies, new practices, new problem domains. The learners keep up. The memorizers fall behind.

What about vibe coding?

Can someone with no training just describe an app and let AI build it?

Today, sometimes yes. For prototypes and simple applications, the results can already be impressive.

But software has a catch: the last stretch is the hardest part. Let's look at it.

The hard 20 percent

Getting to something that looks 80 percent complete may take an afternoon.

The hard part is the remaining 20 percent, where engineering knowledge matters most.

What hides in the last 20
  • Unusual cases
  • Security
  • Scalability
  • Reliability
  • Incorrect data
  • Concurrency
  • Integration
  • Performance
  • Maintenance
  • Failure behavior
A strange paradox

AI lowers the barrier to creating software, and raises the bar for professional engineering.

When AI writes ordinary code instantly, writing ordinary code is worth less. What sets you apart is building systems that are genuinely good.

So, will engineers still write code?

Probably much less of it. The work shifts toward reading, inspecting, and understanding what AI produces.

An open question: can you learn to truly understand code without first writing a lot of it yourself? Nobody has proven it either way. Your generation might find out.

If you are choosing computer science now

🙅 Do not enroll because someone promises "engineers will definitely be safe." Nobody knows that.

🙅 Do not walk away because someone declares "AI will replace all programmers." Nobody knows that either.

You are betting on your own future.
Make it an informed bet.

How to place that bet well

🔍 Do your own research

Watch the industry. Try the newest AI tools yourself. See what they do brilliantly, and where they fall apart.

🧭 Decide what you want to be capable of

Not "which courses do I pass" but "what do I want to be able to do four years from now?"

🔨 Go beyond exercises

Build real, substantial projects. Messy requirements. Hard decisions. Break things, then repair them.

🌊 Depth as well as breadth

AI hands everyone breadth. That makes deep understanding more valuable, not less.

Study the things that last
Algorithms
Data structures
Operating systems
Databases
Networks
Software architecture
Mathematics
Machine learning

Tools change every season. Principles barely move.

One habit that compounds

Practicing what you already do well feels great. It produces comfortable, slow progress.

Growth comes from deliberately working on what you cannot do yet.

The one thing to remember

Do not train to compete with AI at typing code.

Become the person who can:

  • Decide what should be built
  • Understand how systems work
  • Wield powerful tools intelligently
  • Notice when they are wrong
  • Turn an idea into a working product

That is a much bigger definition of a software engineer.

The next decade of software gets built by people who start now.

It could be you.
