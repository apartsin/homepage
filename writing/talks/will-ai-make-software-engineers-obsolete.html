Will AI make software engineers obsolete,
or is now the best time to become one?

The question everyone is asking

Will AI make software engineers obsolete?

Nobody knows.

The technology moves too fast for confident predictions. But we can make an educated guess by looking at:

  • What AI can already do today
  • What happened in earlier technology shifts
But first, what is this job really?

Software is the closest thing we have to a superpower.

💭 You describe an idea, in words or in code, and a machine turns it into something real.

🌍 One engineer with a laptop can build a thing that millions of people use.

💡

Create

You make things that did not exist yesterday. Nothing, then something, from pure thought.

🧩

Solve problems

It is puzzles and detective work. Then the moment it finally clicks and the whole thing comes alive.

🌍

Your software might change the world

Medicine, science, space, how billions of people talk. Software quietly runs the modern world.

Three possible futures
Future 1

Just ask AI

We simply talk to AI directly.

Future 2

AI builds it all

From idea to finished product, autonomously.

Future 3

Engineers essential

The job survives, but changes shape.

Future 1

Why use software at all?

Why click through menus and spreadsheets if you can simply tell an AI what you want?

Future 2

AI builds software autonomously

A user or an analyst describes the need: only what it should do, nothing about how to build it. AI does the rest, end to end: it designs, codes, tests, ships, and maintains.

The code underneath may end up written for machines, not for humans to read.

This is the extreme version of letting AI build the whole thing from a description.

Future 3 · the one many bet on

Engineers stay essential.
The job changes.

AI becomes an indispensable tool and a huge productivity multiplier. But someone still has to decide:

  • What should we build?
  • How should it work?
  • Is the result correct?
  • Is it safe and reliable?
  • Is it actually useful?

AI produces. Engineers decide.

Many people in the industry see this as the most plausible future.

Wait. Cheaper software means fewer jobs, right?

AI makes software cheap to build. What once took ten teams six months, one engineer with a laptop may soon ship in a month.

10× productivity = 10× fewer engineers?

OR

Cheaper software → much more software

  • Today: companies bend themselves to a few ready-made products. Custom software costs too much.
  • If AI slashes that cost: even a tiny company can afford software built for its own way of working.
This already happened once

Once websites became cheap to build, everyone wanted their own custom one.

We did not end up needing fewer websites. Every shop, band, club, and person wanted a site tailored to them. Software may follow the same path.

Think of a small accounting firm. Today it squeezes its work into Excel and the same off-the-shelf programs as everyone else. When custom software becomes cheap, it can have its own system, built around the way that specific firm works.

Another precedent: photography

Photographers did not disappear when digital cameras appeared. Their skills shifted.

Then

A professional once had to master:

  • Film
  • Chemicals
  • Exposure timing
  • Darkroom processing
  • Manual cameras
→ The skill moved.
Now

Digital cameras automated nearly all of that. The craft shifted toward:

  • Composition
  • Lighting
  • Storytelling
  • Selection
  • Artistic judgment
The same shift, for software
Was

What made a strong engineer

  • Mastering one set of tools
  • Writing large amounts of code by hand
  • Memorizing exact commands and syntax
  • Debugging small coding details
Will be

What makes one strong with AI

  • Directing what AI builds
  • Evaluating whether it is right
  • Integrating the pieces into a system
  • Improving what AI produces

The old skills will not vanish, but AI shrinks their relative weight. The engineer moves from typing it all to directing it.

The job gets better, and the bar gets higher

AI takes more of the repetitive work every year. That leaves more of your time for the human part: deciding what to build, designing how it feels, exercising taste and judgment.

But expectations grow too: for what software can do, and for the engineers who build it.

Code becomes abundant. Judgment becomes scarce.

So what will make an engineer strong?

Four skills worth building

Not a survival checklist. These are what let one creative person build what used to take a whole team.

01

Breadth

One person now covers what once took several specialists.

02

Communication

Say precisely what should be built.

03

Judgment

Evaluate work in territory you have never seen.

04

Learning

Today's tools are not next year's tools.

01
Skill 01 · Breadth

One head, many hats

An engineer may need to think, at the same time, about many areas. You do not need to be world-class in all of them: just enough to recognize what matters, and notice when something is wrong. That reach is the fun part: you get to build the whole thing.

  • Architecture
  • Databases
  • User experience
  • Testing
  • Security
  • Shipping
  • Performance
  • AI
  • Product
02
Skill 02 · Clear technical communication

What, exactly, are we trying to build?

You will communicate with AI systems, customers, managers, designers, and other engineers. Precision is the job.

In an AI-assisted world, unclear wording quickly becomes unclear software.

03
Skill 03 · Judgment under uncertainty

Deciding in a messy world of technologies

Moving fast, with partial knowledge, and still making good calls.

AI can hand you solutions built with technologies you have never seen. You need enough fundamentals and breadth to:

  1. Understand roughly how it works
  2. Notice suspicious assumptions
  3. Compare alternatives
  4. Know when to dig deeper

Making good decisions when little information is available takes experience, solid fundamentals, and breadth. Gut feeling is not a guess. It is years of experience and deep knowledge, compressed into a second.

04
Skill 04 · Fast learning

The tools you learn today may be gone in five years.
Maybe two.

So the ability to learn quickly becomes a central professional skill: new AI tools, new technologies, new practices, new problem domains. What you can learn matters more than what you can memorize.

Can anyone build software now?

Sometimes, yes. Someone with no engineering training can describe an app and let AI build it. (People call this "vibe coding.") But software has a catch: the hardest part often comes last.

Getting to something that looks 80 percent complete may take an afternoon. For some applications, that is genuinely enough.

The hard part is the last stretch, where engineering knowledge matters most. Would you board a plane running vibe-coded software?

What hides in the last stretch
  • Strange cases you did not expect
  • Security problems
  • Many users at once
  • Bad or missing data
  • Connections to other systems
  • Speed problems
  • Failures
  • Long-term maintenance
So, will engineers still write code?

Probably much less of it. The work shifts toward reading, inspecting, and understanding what AI produces.

An open question: can you learn to truly understand code without first writing a lot of it yourself? Nobody has proven it either way. Your generation might find out.

How to get the most out of your computer science studies

🔍 Research industry trends and the job market

Research where the industry is going and which skills are in demand. Try the newest AI tools yourself: see what they do brilliantly, and where they fall apart.

🧭 Set your target skill set

Not "which courses do I pass" but "what do I want to be able to do three years from now?"

🔨 Go beyond exercises: build real experience

Build real, substantial projects. Messy requirements. Hard decisions. Break things, then repair them.

🌊 Stand out through depth

With AI, anyone can cover many areas at a basic level. What sets you apart is deep understanding: that is what prepares you for AI-assisted engineering. Take the core courses seriously.
